Exercise Library

A movement database optimised for mechanical tension and joint alignment.

Incline Dumbbell Curl

Target: Biceps Brachii (Long Head Focus)

Set a bench to a 45-degree incline. Let your arms hang straight down behind your torso. Keep your elbows locked in place as you curl the dumbbells upward, supinating your wrists at the top.

Biomechanics Note: Positioning the shoulder into hyperextension places the long head of the biceps into a passive stretch. Research shows training muscles at long muscle lengths significantly boosts hypertrophy.

Overhead Dumbbell Triceps Extension

Target: Triceps Brachii (Long Head Focus)

Sit or stand upright holding a dumbbell overhead with both hands. Lower the weight slowly behind your head by bending at the elbows, keeping your upper arms vertical and close to your ears, then press back up to full extension.

Biomechanics Note: Because the long head of the triceps crosses the shoulder joint, raising your arms overhead places this specific head into a state of maximal stretch. Training the triceps in this position leverages stretch-mediated hypertrophy for superior muscle growth compared to neutral arm positions.

Romanian Deadlift (RDL)

Target: Hamstrings & Glutes (Lengthened State Focus)

Stand with feet hip-width apart holding a barbell or dumbbells. Initiate the movement by pushing your hips backward, keeping your spine neutral and knees slightly unlocked. Lower the weight along your shins until you feel a deep stretch in your hamstrings, then drive through your hips to return to upright.

Biomechanics Note: Unlike standard deadlifts, the RDL keeps constant tension on the posterior chain by avoiding a floor reset. It isolates the hip hinge, loading the hamstrings heavily eccentric-wise at their longest length, which triggers massive mechanical tension and structural remodeling.

Bulgarian Split Squat

Target: Quadriceps & Gluteus Maximus (Unilateral Bias)

Place one foot flat on the floor and elevate your rear foot on a bench behind you. Holding dumbbells at your sides, lower your hips until your back knee is just above the floor and your front thigh is parallel to the ground, then drive through your front heel to stand.

Biomechanics Note: Unilateral exercises eliminate bilateral deficits, ensuring your dominant side doesn't compensate for your weaker side. The deep hip flexion achieved at the bottom of the movement places the gluteus maximus under a powerful loaded stretch, maximizing recruitment.

Incline Dumbbell Flye

Target: Pectoralis Major (Clavicular Head Focus)

Lie on a bench set to a 30-degree incline holding dumbbells overhead with palms facing. Slowly lower your arms out to the sides in a wide arc while keeping a slight bend in your elbows until you feel a deep stretch across your upper chest, then reverse the motion.

Biomechanics Note: Setting the bench to a slight incline aligns the force vector with the horizontal, upward-running fibers of the upper clavicular head of the chest. The wide arc creates a long moment arm that maximizes torque and loaded mechanical tension at the bottom stretch of the movement.

Barbell Preacher Curl

Target: Biceps Brachii (Short Head Focus)

Sit at a preacher bench and rest the back of your upper arms flat against the sloped pad. Hold a barbell with an underhand grip, lower the weight until your arms are fully extended, and curl the bar upward toward your chin while keeping your armpits firmly locked against the pad.

Biomechanics Note: Placing the shoulders into passive flexion shifts the neural drive and mechanical load heavily onto the short head of the biceps. Additionally, the fixed incline of the pad prevents the shoulders from moving, completely eliminating cheating and momentum.

Seated Machine Leg Curl

Target: Hamstrings (High-Length Knee Flexion)

Sit firmly in the machine with the back of your lower legs resting on the padded roller. Secure the thigh pad tightly against your lap, pull your toes toward your shins, and powerfully flex your knees to draw the pad back and under your seat.

Biomechanics Note: Because the hamstrings cross both the hip and knee joints, sitting upright keeps the hips flexed, placing the muscles into a pre-stretched state. Training knee flexion from this position yields significantly greater hypertrophy than lying down, due to the high-stretch tension dynamics.

Chest-Supported Dumbbell Row

Target: Latissimus Dorsi & Rhomboids (Concentric Contraction Focus)

Set an incline bench to roughly 30 degrees and lie prone with your chest supported. Fully extend your arms with dumbbells, then pull your elbows back towards your hips, squeezing your shoulder blades together at the peak of the movement.

Biomechanics Note: Supporting the torso completely eliminates momentum and lower back axial loading. This isolation allows you to safely train to absolute muscular failure, pulling the elbows past the torso into deep glenohumeral extension for maximum latissimus contraction.

Behind-the-Back Cable Lateral Raise

Target: Lateral Deltoid (Constant Tension Focus)

Set a low cable pulley, stand facing away from it, and reach behind your back to grab the handle. Keeping a slight bend in your elbow, raise your arm out to the side until it reaches shoulder height, then slowly resist the weight back down.

Biomechanics Note: Unlike dumbbells, which offer zero resistance at the bottom of a lateral raise, the cable pulley creates a perpendicular line of pull. This maintains constant mechanical tension on the lateral deltoid even at its most lengthened position, accelerating muscle damage and growth.

Standing Machine Calf Raise

Target: Gastrocnemius (Straight-Knee Bias)

Adjust the shoulder pads of the machine to your height and place the balls of your feet on the platform edge. Lower your heels as far as possible to feel a deep calf stretch, then explode upward onto your toes, holding the contraction for a full second.

Biomechanics Note: The gastrocnemius is a biarticular muscle crossing both the knee and ankle joints. By keeping the knees completely locked straight during the raise, you prevent passive insufficiency and maximize the mechanical tension directly on the gastrocnemius, rather than the deeper soleus muscle.
